# Programming decisions

Use for constructing sessions, substitutions, progression, cardio, and reviews. These are adjustable coaching defaults for generally healthy adults, not medical rehabilitation protocols or claims that one program is optimal for everyone.

## Build the smallest effective week

Start from ranked goals, recent tolerated work, available days/minutes, equipment, and limitations. For a beginner with two or three days, full-body strength is often a practical starting structure; preserve a productive user-selected split. Cover major muscle groups across the week without forcing every movement into every session.

Count only completed working sets in volume reviews. Separate warm-ups and direct work from overlapping compound work, state the counting convention, and avoid counting every compound as a full direct set for every involved muscle. Missing logs make volume a lower bound, not proof that no training occurred.

Consistency and individual fit matter more than complex programming. ACSM's healthy-adult guidance supports training major muscle groups at least twice weekly, with load and volume tailored to goals; routine failure training is not required. Use a modest starting dose and build from recovery/performance rather than imposing an advanced volume target on a novice. [ACSM 2026 summary](https://www.acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf)

Practical starting options, adjusted to the user:

- New/returning lifter: 1–3 working sets per movement, controlled reps, usually around 2–3 clean reps in reserve (RIR). Start below prior peak volume after a layoff.
- Hypertrophy emphasis: commonly 6–15 reps, using a suitable range for the exercise; enough rest to repeat quality work (often 2–3 minutes on demanding compounds, 1–2 minutes on smaller movements).
- Strength emphasis for a prepared lifter: prioritize specific main lifts earlier, lower rep ranges such as 3–6, smaller jumps, and longer rests (often 3–5 minutes). Do not require a true 1RM test to set a starting load.

These ranges are coaching starting points, not mandatory thresholds. Fit the time budget including ramp-up sets, transitions, and rests. If time shrinks, retain the highest-priority work and trim optional sets/accessories; do not remove needed rest just to fit an inflated exercise list.

Place the highest-priority or most technically demanding work while fresh. When strength and hard cardio share a day, order them around the primary goal and explain the fatigue tradeoff; separate demanding lower-body work and intervals when feasible. Rebalance missed work over the remaining schedule rather than doubling the next session.

## Prescribe loads without pretending to know them

Reuse a verified comparable load when available. Otherwise give a concrete calibration procedure: start light, perform a controlled trial set in the intended rep range, and adjust by the smallest available increment to finish with the planned clean reps in reserve. For an unfamiliar lift, prioritize technique and stop well before failure. Record that first result as a baseline.

Define RIR once if unfamiliar: “2 RIR” means roughly two more clean reps were possible. Treat the estimate as subjective, especially for beginners. User words such as “easy” inform the next decision but do not justify inventing a precise RIR history. Do not assume a percentage of body weight, an estimated max, or a machine setting is a known safe load.

## Compare, then progress

Before interpreting a change, match exercise, setup/range of motion, machine, units/convention, set count, exercise order, effort, rest, and preceding fatigue as far as records allow. Classify a new setup as a baseline, and sparse/ambiguous data as insufficient for a trend. Use “below the comparable reference today” rather than diagnosing regression from one bad set.

| Observation | Next decision |
| --- | --- |
| More clean reps at the same load, sets, and similar effort | Rep progression; keep building within the prescribed range. |
| Every working set reaches the top of the range with intended effort and stable form | Use the smallest practical load increase next exposure; expect reps near the lower end. If the increment is too large, keep the load and build control/reps or use a smaller increment if available. |
| Only the first set reaches the top; later sets fall short | Hold load; improve repeatability, rest, or pacing. Do not progress from the best set alone. |
| Target reps achieved only at failure or with form loss | Do not auto-increase. Repeat with control, lower load, or adjust sets/rest. |
| A single poor exposure after unusual fatigue or changed order | Address context and reassess; do not immediately replace a productive exercise. |
| Roughly 2–3 comparable exposures stall | Check technique, rest, effort, jump size, recovery, and weekly dose; change the smallest likely limiting factor. This is a review trigger, not proof a new exercise is needed. |
| Several lifts repeatedly fall alongside persistent fatigue | Consider a brief reduction in sets and/or load, easier cardio, and reassessment. Do not diagnose overtraining from a wearable score. |
| Pain or a new restriction | Stop the provoking movement; follow the safety guidance in SKILL.md before progression. |

Example: at `3 × 6–10`, `10/8/7` does not earn more weight. `10/10/10` with the intended reserve and stable technique can. The next load depends on available increments; never silently round to equipment the user lacks.

For assisted pull-ups/dips, compare assistance and bodyweight context explicitly. For different machines, establish a new baseline instead of converting stack numbers into an equivalent free-weight load. Preserve original units when deriving a labeled conversion. Volume-load and estimated 1RM, when useful, are derived measures with limitations; neither is automatically a PR or a measure of muscle gained.

## Substitute the stimulus

Match movement pattern, primary muscles, tolerable range, stability demands, and fatigue cost to available equipment. Explain the most relevant tradeoff. An occupied machine may justify changing order; a painful movement requires a non-provoking option and symptom clarification. Neither justifies copying a load across machines.

If dumbbells are too light, adjust reps or use an appropriate unilateral variation before adding complexity. Preserve actual order and the substitution in the log. Keep exercise names stable and record changed setup separately so future comparisons can find the right reference.

## Aerobic work and VO₂

General adult public-health guidance is 150 minutes of moderate or 75 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity weekly, or an equivalent mix, plus muscle-strengthening work at least twice weekly. This is a direction to build toward, not a first-week quota. Inactive users start with tolerable activity and progress gradually. Moderate effort usually allows talking but not singing; vigorous effort limits speech to a few words. [CDC activity guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/adding-adults/what-counts.html)

For easy aerobic/“Zone 2” work, use comfortable conversational effort and a modality/duration the user can repeat. Zone labels differ across devices and testing systems; do not equate a watch zone with a measured physiological threshold. Use individualized tested zones when available, otherwise the talk test and perceived effort with explicit uncertainty. Avoid precise heart-rate targets derived solely from an age formula, especially when medication affects heart rate.

For VO₂-priority users, introduce dedicated intervals when recent aerobic tolerance and recovery support them. They are an option, not a requirement for every general-health user. A new or detrained user may start with easy aerobic sessions or shorter controlled work bouts; do not prescribe a full hard 4 × 4 solely because the goal mentions longevity.

For a prepared user, one useful session is a 10-minute progressive warm-up, four 4-minute hard but repeatable bouts, 3 minutes easy between bouts, and a 5-minute cool-down: **40 minutes total**. Aim for controlled vigorous work, not all-out sprints; pace must be repeatable through the final bout. The 4 × 4 structure and recovery timing are drawn from [NTNU's CERG program](https://www.ntnu.edu/cerg/regimen).

Start with a frequency the existing week can absorb, often one interval session while establishing tolerance. Progress one variable at a time based on completed work and recovery: execution/repeatability, then work duration or number of bouts, or pace/power where appropriate. Moving from four to five bouts is optional, not an inevitable goal. Do not increase frequency, duration, and intensity together. If quality collapses, slow down, shorten, or stop the work rather than reward suffering. Apply the stop conditions in SKILL.md.

Log actual bouts, work/recovery time, modality, and pace/power/HR only when known. Record comparable improvements in duration, pace or power at similar effort separately from a measured VO₂ change. Apple Watch cardio fitness is an estimate derived from eligible outdoor walking/running/hiking activity; a missing update after indoor cycling does not show a failed session or no fitness improvement. [Apple cardio fitness documentation](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108790)

## Review and adaptation

When asked for a review or when repeated stalls justify one, compare a stated period with the prior comparable period. Report:

- completed versus intended sessions, with missing-data caveats;
- a few meaningful lift and cardio trends with their actual baselines;
- weekly direct-set estimates and aerobic minutes only where logs support them;
- repeated pain, changed setup, or recovery constraints;
- the smallest useful plan adjustment and when/how to judge its effect.

Do not turn adherence into a moral judgment or promise adaptations from one workout. Preserve what is working. Change emphasis when the user changes goals without rewriting history or creating another workbook.
